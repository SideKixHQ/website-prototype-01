How to Get Help Starting a Business: Where to Actually Look

Where to actually get help starting a business: free SBA-funded mentoring through SCORE and SBDCs, local resources, peer accountability and paid advisors.

Published 20 August 2026 Reading time 9 minutes Category Community

Most people who want to start a business never do. Not because the idea was bad, and not because they lacked the ability — but because they got stuck somewhere between "I should do something about this" and "I know what to do next," and nobody was there to close the gap.

The numbers on this are stark. In a Harris Poll conducted for Zapier, 61% of Americans said they had come up with at least one business idea — and 92% of them never followed through (The Harris Poll for Zapier, survey of 2,001 U.S. adults, December 2020). That is not a talent problem. That is a support problem.

The good news is that there is far more help available than most people realise, and a surprising amount of it is free. The bad news is that it is scattered across dozens of organisations with confusing acronyms, and nobody hands you a map. This guide is that map.

First, work out what kind of help you actually need

"I need help starting a business" is too vague to act on. Before you contact anyone, it is worth spending ten minutes working out which of these four things is actually blocking you, because each one has a different solution and asking the wrong source wastes weeks.

1. Information — you don't know the rules

You don't know whether you need an LLC, what licences your city requires, how sales tax works, or whether you can legally run this from your kitchen. This is the easiest gap to close and the cheapest. It is a research problem, not an advice problem.

2. Structure — you don't know the order of operations

You know roughly what needs to happen, but not what comes first. Should you register the business before you have a customer? Build the website before you validate the idea? This is the most common form of stuckness and the most under-served, because it feels too basic to ask about. It isn't.

3. Accountability — you know what to do and aren't doing it

Nothing is unclear. You simply keep not doing it, because there are no consequences for not doing it and no one is expecting anything from you. This is not a knowledge gap and no amount of research will fix it. What fixes it is another human being expecting something from you on a date.

4. Expertise — you need someone who has done this specific thing

You have a real, specific, technical question: how to price a wholesale line, how to structure a partnership agreement, how to hire your first employee legally. Generic advice actively hurts here. You need someone who has done this exact thing in this exact industry.

Be honest about which one you are. Most people who say they need expertise actually need structure, and most people who say they need more information actually need accountability. Our guide to what to do after having your business idea walks through diagnosing this properly.

Free help, funded by the U.S. government

This is the most underused resource in American entrepreneurship. The Small Business Administration funds a network of partner organisations that provide counselling and training at no cost. Not discounted — free. Most people have never heard of them.

SCORE

SCORE is a network of volunteer business mentors, most of them retired or working executives and business owners. You are matched with a mentor and can meet by video, phone or email, for free, for as long as you need. There is no session limit and no upsell, because there is nothing to sell.

SCORE is strongest for the structure and accountability gaps. A good SCORE mentor will make you articulate what you are actually doing, then hold you to it across months. It is weakest when you need deep, current, industry-specific expertise — the volunteer pool is broad but not infinite.

Best for: first-time founders who need a steady, experienced human to think out loud with. score.org

Small Business Development Centers (SBDCs)

SBDCs are hosted at universities and state economic development agencies, and staffed by paid professional advisors rather than volunteers. They provide free counselling on business planning, financial management, marketing, access to capital and exporting.

Because they are professionally staffed and often university-affiliated, SBDCs tend to be stronger than SCORE on the technical and financial side — particularly if you are preparing to apply for a loan. Many will help you build and stress-test the financial projections a lender will demand.

Best for: anyone who needs a real business plan, financial projections, or is preparing to borrow money. Find your local SBDC

Women's Business Centers

A national network offering free to low-cost counselling and training, with a specific focus on women entrepreneurs and on communities that have historically had less access to capital and networks. Services cover launch and growth, federal contracting, and access to capital.

Veterans Business Outreach Centers

Free workshops, training, counselling and mentorship for service members, veterans and military spouses. Covers business plan development, financial review, and specialised areas including franchising and international trade.

All four networks are searchable through the SBA's local assistance tool. If you do nothing else from this article, spend twenty minutes finding out which of these exist within driving distance of you.

Local help that costs nothing or very little

Your public library

This sounds like a joke and is not. Many public library systems hold subscriptions to market research databases — industry reports, demographic data, competitor databases — that cost thousands of dollars a year commercially and are free with a library card. Larger systems often have a dedicated business librarian whose entire job is helping people find exactly this kind of information.

If your blocker is the information gap, this is frequently the fastest route through it.

Chambers of commerce

Membership usually costs a few hundred dollars a year. What you are buying is not the newsletter — it is proximity to other local business owners who have already solved the problems you are about to hit, and who know which local accountant, lawyer and printer are actually good.

Incubators and accelerators

Worth understanding the difference. Incubators give early-stage businesses space, resources and mentorship over a long, loosely structured period, often for free or cheap, sometimes for equity. Accelerators run fixed-length, intensive cohort programmes, usually take equity, and are generally aimed at businesses with high-growth ambitions. If you are opening a bakery, an accelerator is the wrong shape. An incubator might not be.

Peer help: the category most people skip

There is a particular kind of help that no expert can provide, which is the help of someone at roughly your stage going through roughly your thing at roughly the same time. Experts give you answers. Peers give you calibration — the knowledge that what you are experiencing is normal, which is often the thing that stops people quitting.

This matters more than it sounds. Isolation is one of the most reliable predictors of founders giving up, and it is almost entirely preventable. We have written about the loneliness of entrepreneurship and how to stop feeling isolated as a small business owner in more depth.

Accountability partners

The single highest-leverage, lowest-cost intervention available to a stuck founder. One other person, a standing weekly call, and a specific commitment each week. That is the entire mechanism, and it outperforms most paid coaching for people whose real problem is the accountability gap.

If you want the structure rather than having to invent it, we publish a free accountability partner template and a weekly business check-in template.

Masterminds and founder communities

A small group meeting regularly, each member bringing real problems for the group to work on. Paid masterminds can be excellent and can also be expensive theatre — the differentiator is whether members bring genuine problems or performances. Ask to observe a session before paying.

Networking, done properly

Most networking advice is bad because it treats networking as collection. The useful version is narrow: find the ten people who have done the specific thing you are trying to do, and ask each of them one specific question. See how to network when you're just starting out and our networking outreach template.

Paid help: what you get and roughly what it costs

At some point free help runs out, usually when your questions get specific enough that generic answers stop working. Here is what the paid landscape actually looks like.

AI tools: useful for the information gap, useless for the rest

AI assistants are genuinely excellent at the information gap. They will explain business structures, draft a first-pass plan, summarise regulations and rewrite your copy, instantly and nearly free.

They are much weaker on the other three gaps. They cannot hold you accountable, because they do not notice or care when you disappear for three weeks. They cannot supply calibration, because they have never been scared. And their expertise is generic by construction — confidently phrased, occasionally wrong, and with no stake in whether you succeed.

Use them for research, drafting and thinking out loud. Do not mistake them for a support system. Our roundup of the best AI tools for small business owners covers what they are actually good at.

How to choose — and what good help looks like

Map the gap to the source and you will save yourself months:

  • Information gap → library, SBA resources, AI tools
  • Structure gap → SCORE, SBDC, a structured programme
  • Accountability gap → accountability partner, peer group, coach
  • Expertise gap → a paid advisor or consultant in that specific domain

Good help is specific, honest and leaves you more capable. It tells you what will not work. It shrinks over time as you outgrow it.

Bad help is generic, flattering and creates dependency. Some reliable warning signs: pressure to decide today, guaranteed outcomes, a refusal to name past clients, fees that scale with your enthusiasm rather than the work, and advice that never once contradicts what you wanted to hear.

The one thing that actually matters

Every resource above is real, available, and mostly free. None of it works if you do not contact anyone.

The 92% who never act are not lazy and they are not short of options. They are alone with a decision, and alone is the condition under which decisions do not get made. The intervention is not more information. It is one other person who knows what you are trying to do and expects to hear how it went.

Pick one thing from this page. Book the SCORE session, message the person you have been meaning to message, or ask one friend to be your accountability partner. Do it today, while this is still in front of you.

Frequently asked questions

Where can I get free help starting a business?

SCORE and your local Small Business Development Center both offer free, ongoing business counselling funded through the U.S. Small Business Administration. Women's Business Centers and Veterans Business Outreach Centers serve specific groups on the same free-to-low-cost basis. Your public library is also a free source of paid-tier market research.

Do I need a mentor or a coach?

A mentor if you need perspective and judgement from someone who has been where you are going, usually over a long period and usually unpaid. A coach if you know what to do and are not doing it — coaching is structured accountability. See how to find a business mentor.

How much does business help cost?

SBA-funded counselling is free. Chamber membership typically runs a few hundred dollars a year. Paid coaching and advisory services vary enormously by market and seniority. Start free, and only pay when you have a question free help genuinely cannot answer.

What if I don't know what my business idea is yet?

That is a legitimate starting point, not a disqualification. Begin with turning your existing skills into a business and small business ideas for beginners, then work through the questions worth answering before you commit.
