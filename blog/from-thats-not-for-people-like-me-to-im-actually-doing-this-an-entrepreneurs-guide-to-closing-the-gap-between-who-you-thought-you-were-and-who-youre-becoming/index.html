From "That's Not for People Like Me" to "I'm Actually Doing This" An entrepreneur's guide to closing the gap between who you thought you were and who you're becoming.

A guide for first-time entrepreneurs on closing the gap between who you thought you were and who you’re becoming.

Published 24 June 2026 Reading time 4 minutes Category Advisors

The Gap Has a Name

There’s a moment most first-time entrepreneurs experience, usually quietly, often alone, where they catch themselves doing something they once filed away under “not for people like me.”

Maybe you’re on a call with an investor. Maybe you’re presenting to your first real customer. Maybe you just registered your LLC and stared at the confirmation email for a little longer than necessary.

That gap between the person who once thought this was impossible and the person now doing it is not a small thing. It’s actually one of the most important realizations in your journey. It means the story you told yourself about your own limits is starting to crack.

This guide is about understanding that gap, why it forms, and how to use it as fuel rather than letting it quietly undermine you.

Why the Gap Exists in the First Place

The belief that certain things aren’t “for you” doesn’t come from nowhere. It usually has very specific origins.

It comes from representation

If you’ve never seen someone who looks like you, grew up where you did, or started with what you had build something real, your brain files “builder” or “entrepreneur” or even “musician” or “actor” — you get the point. That is not you. That is someone else’s category.

It comes from proximity

Entrepreneurship used to feel like a closed room. You either knew someone on the inside or you didn’t. Many first-time entrepreneurs simply didn’t grow up near that room.

It comes from accumulated small moments

A teacher who redirected your ambition (Thank you Mr. Lowell). A family that equated risk with irresponsibility. A culture that celebrated security over creation. None of these people meant harm, but the message accumulated anyway.

Understanding where the gap came from isn’t about blame, it’s about recognizing that the belief was installed, not discovered. And anything installed can be uninstalled. Restart, Reset, Reinstall.

What’s Actually Happening When You Cross the Gap

When you catch yourself doing the thing you thought wasn’t possible, a few things are happening simultaneously.

Your identity is updating

Psychologists call this identity-behavior alignment. You’ve taken enough action that your self-concept is quietly rewriting itself. You’re not pretending to be a founder. You are one. The label is catching up to reality.

Your evidence base is shifting

You used to have zero proof that you could do this. Now you have some. Every call you got on, every problem you solved, every day you didn’t quit — that’s evidence your former self didn’t have access to.

Your risk tolerance is recalibrating

What felt terrifying six months ago now feels manageable. Not because it got easier, but because you got more capable. The gap between your current skills and the challenge in front of you has narrowed.

This is growth — and it’s worth pausing to name it clearly, rather than rushing past it to the next problem.

How to Work With the Gap (Not Against It)

The gap between who you were and who you’re becoming is a resource. Here’s how to actively use it.

1. Document the Before

If you haven’t already, write down, right now, what you believed about yourself before you started. What did you think you couldn’t do? What category did you put yourself in? What was the specific story?

Keep it somewhere you can return to. In the future you will need it on hard days.

2. Track the Crossings

Every time you do something you once thought wasn’t possible, note it. Not in a braggadocious public way — in a private, honest record of evidence. This becomes your personal case file against the old narrative.

Small examples are just as valid as big ones. Your first cold email sent. Your first “no” survived. Your first pivot made without falling apart.

3. Name the Old Voice, Don’t Argue With It

When the old belief resurfaces — and it will — don’t try to logic your way out of it in the moment. Instead, name it. “There’s that old story again.” Then return to your evidence file.

Arguing with the voice gives it energy. Naming it and redirecting works better.

4. Find Your Proof-of-Concept People

Seek out an entrepreneur whose starting point looks something like yours. Not because their path is your path, but because their existence proves the category is larger than you were told. Representation isn’t just inspiring, it’s connecting, it’s empowering, it’s proof.

James Martucci, CEO, SideKix

Frequently asked questions

What is the identity gap in entrepreneurship?

It is the distance between who you currently believe you are and who you would have to be to run the business you are imagining. The article treats it as a normal feature of starting rather than a sign that you have picked the wrong thing.

Why does starting a business feel like it is for other people?

Because most people build their sense of what they are capable of from what they have already done. A first business has no precedent in your own history, so the mind files it under other people.

How long does it take to feel like a business owner?

The article describes it as arriving after the fact rather than before. The identity tends to follow the evidence, which means doing the thing first and feeling like the kind of person who does it later.

Can you close the gap before you start?

Waiting for the feeling to arrive first is the trap the article describes, since the feeling is produced by action rather than preceding it.
